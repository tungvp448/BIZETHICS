    Our story

    How a project about children with cancer became a project about the mothers who care for them, and about every mother.

    The group we noticed

    We first went to K Hospital to help sick children. What we found was a bigger, quieter problem: the care work of mothers, unpaid and unseen, which is at its most extreme there but exists in every home. In the ward, the carers of sick children, most often their mothers, are the link that holds the whole system of care together. They stay in the ward for months, give up their income, and put their own needs last.

    Yet public attention and charity focus almost entirely on the child, and so overlook an equally vulnerable group: the mothers.

    What the research says

    This is not only our impression. The Standards of Psychosocial Care for Parents of Children with Cancer make a strong recommendation that parents receive early and ongoing mental-health support. The authors reviewed 138 studies. They found that parents’ distress peaks around diagnosis, and that a distressed parent can affect how the child copes (Kearney et al., 2015).

    This matters for the child too, but it is not the main reason. Carers deserve care and recognition for their own sake: their work is real, unpaid and unseen.

    The people we met

    While contacting different stakeholders for support, we met Ms. X and Ms. Y. These two independent women started a volunteer group for patients and parents of children with cancer after a family member of their own had cancer. Having gone through the same story with them, we were delighted to work with them, gain access to the ward, and listen to what the families actually needed.

    Why it felt so close to home

    After listening to the mothers’ stories, we kept coming back to one thought: they reminded us of our own mothers. Everything these mothers sacrificed, our own mothers sacrifice too: time, youth, passion, her life. It is everywhere, in everyone, yet no one sees it.

    This widened our view beyond the group we first set out to help.

    Care for the Carers

    We also realised that lasting change will not come from one visit or one donation, but from a shift in how everyone treats the carers in their own lives. We can either keep helping one ward at a time, or change how society treats carers everywhere. We chose to do both.

    That is why our main track is a social media campaign, “Care for the Carers”: saying thank you this 20/10 to the women who have taken care of us. Alongside it runs our K Hospital track, funding the mothers who carry the heaviest form of care work, as live proof that carers can be supported and paid.

    In this project, we tell the stories of the mothers who most need help, through their own eyes in the hospital. We then apply what we learnt from them to design a more systematic change that encourages everyone to recognise and care for the carers in their own lives, in the hope of creating a domino effect that encourages everyone to care for carers of every kind.

    Your turn. Think of the woman who took care of you. It takes one minute to say thank you.
